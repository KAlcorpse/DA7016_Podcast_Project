# Make Every Podcast Line Prove Its Source

The papers that matter most for this project are about grounding, not podcasts. Huang et al. show that an LLM checking its own output without an outside signal makes answers worse. MiniCheck and Vectara's HHEM show that a checker under 1B parameters, small enough to run on a laptop CPU, matches GPT-4-class judges on grounding benchmarks. "Attribute First, then Generate" shows that tying each sentence to source spans before writing makes every later check cheaper and more accurate. Together they turn "never use the model's memory of NCERT" from a prompt instruction into a check that every line must pass. Next in priority are cost levers, and these come from provider documentation rather than papers. DeepSeek on OpenRouter bills cached prompt reads at **0.1x** the normal input price. A fixed, chapter-first prompt prefix therefore removes most of the cost of resending the chapter on every call. Switching off reasoning tokens for extraction, writing and fixing removes billed output those tasks don't need. The learning-science papers then say the two-host format only teaches when Host A asks deep why/how questions and voices misconceptions that Host B refutes (effect size about **d = 0.8** in Muller et al.). On its own, a generic AI podcast tested no better than reading the textbook (PAIGE). Your quiz checkpoints rest on the strongest evidence in the whole review: classroom quizzing raises learning by about **g = 0.5** overall and about **0.6–0.65 in middle and high school**, with larger effects when feedback is given. Always showing the correct answer and why is the cheapest learning gain available. On the audio side, Indic-Speak's own documentation says it can repeat or drop content and silently falls back to a degraded voice if given an unknown voice name, so a per-line ASR listen-back check and a `spoken_text` with no digits or symbols are mandatory. The weakness running through all of this is that almost no study used Indian school students, NCERT text or Indian-language TTS, so the product's own quiz logs will have to become its evidence base.

## A sub-1B checker plus cited evidence beats an LLM grading its own script

The project's core promise is that the hosts say only what the chapter says. It is also the riskiest promise, because the evidence consistently says a model grading its own output is a weak safeguard. Huang et al. found that self-correction with no external signal lowered accuracy. GPT-4 on HotpotQA fell from **49.0 to 43.0** over two rounds, and GPT-3.5 on CommonSenseQA dropped from 75.8 to 38.1. The gains reported in earlier work came from oracle labels telling the model when to stop ([Huang et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2310.01798v2)). Kamoi et al.'s TACL survey reached the same verdict. Self-correction works with reliable external feedback or after large-scale fine-tuning. No prior work shows it working with feedback from a prompted LLM, except on unusually suitable tasks ([Kamoi et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.01297)). Your current shape, a separate verifier followed by a fix call, is the right one. It works only if the verifier really is a different signal from the writer, and only if no LLM call is ever allowed to declare its own fix acceptable. Huang et al. also found that a better initial prompt beat self-correction (**81.8 vs 75.1** on CommonGen-Hard). So the source-only rule and the analogy rule belong in the dialogue-writing prompt itself, not just in stages 4–5.

### Attach evidence IDs during extraction so verification becomes a short-span check

Slobodkin et al. split generation into content selection, sentence planning and sentence writing, so the selected source segments become fine-grained citations. They got more concise attribution at similar or better quality, and human assessors verified facts faster ([Attribute First](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.17104)). Your fact-extraction stage already does content selection, so the change is mostly plumbing. Give every fact a stable ID, its verbatim source sentence(s) and character offsets. Have the plan list which fact IDs each segment covers. Have every factual script line emit `evidence_ids`. Verification then compares a one-sentence line against a two-sentence premise rather than a whole section, which is cheaper and more accurate. It falls back to the full section only on failure, to catch lines that are correct but cite the wrong ID.

ALCE shows why this has to be enforced rather than hoped for: on ELI5, even the best models lacked complete citation support **50% of the time** ([ALCE](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.14627)). Have the verifier output citation recall (is the line entailed by its cited spans?) and citation precision (is each cited span needed?). A side benefit is that `evidence_ids` let the transcript show "from the chapter: …" when a student taps a line.

The same schema should carry a `line_type`. VeriScore's central point is that not every sentence is a verifiable claim ([VeriScore](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.19276)), and a podcast mixes facts with questions, banter and analogies. Use `fact`, `refutation` and `recap` lines for content that must be entailed by its cited spans. Mark `deep_question` and `banter` lines as ones that must contain no factual claim. Check `analogy` lines for "no new verifiable claim": a VeriScore-style claim extractor should return an empty list. Treat `misconception` lines as deliberately false, and verify that a cited refutation follows within two turns rather than verifying the misconception itself. Without these types, the verifier either flags every analogy and planted misconception as unsupported, or learns to let them through. FaithDial adds one more rule. In document-grounded dialogue, most hallucination comes from "personality": first-person anecdotes, opinions stated as facts and invented examples. So forbid those outright ([FaithDial](https://aclanthology.org/2022.tacl-1.84/)).

### Let a sub-1B checker screen every line and escalate only the doubtful ones

MiniCheck trains small checkers on GPT-4-generated synthetic errors. The 770M Flan-T5 variant reaches GPT-4-level accuracy at about **400x lower cost**, and it does so without a separate claim-decomposition step ([MiniCheck](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.10774v2)). On the LLM-AggreFact leaderboard, MiniCheck-Flan-T5-L scores **75.0**, against 75.9 for GPT-4o and 77.2 for Claude-3.5 Sonnet ([leaderboard](https://llm-aggrefact.github.io/)). HHEM-2.1-Open is smaller still: a roughly 0.1B Flan-T5-base model under Apache-2.0 that runs in **under 600MB of RAM** and takes about 1.5 s for a 2k-token input on a CPU. It beats zero-shot GPT-4 on AggreFact-SOTA balanced accuracy (76.55 vs 73.78). It is also asymmetric by design: it flags claims that are true but unsupported, and its own example flags "Paris" when the source says "Berlin" ([HHEM card](https://huggingface.co/vectara/hallucination_evaluation_model)). That asymmetry is exactly the "no memory" rule. A line that is correct from the model's training but absent from the chapter should fail, and HHEM fails it. Neither checker is available on OpenRouter, so this means a small self-hosted Python step. HHEM's CPU footprint is what makes that realistic on a student budget.

Run the checker on each factual line against its cited spans, and pass the previous one or two turns along with the line. Do not split lines into atomic claims by default. Hu et al. found that decomposition raised a weak verifier's accuracy slightly, but cut MiniCheck's from **80.01 to between 71.11 and 76.81** on WICE, and hurt more on short, simple inputs ([Decomposition Dilemmas](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.02400v2)). Most podcast lines are short. Their top decomposition errors were lost context and ambiguous pronouns, and dialogue lines like "Exactly, and that's why it dissolves" are full of both. Decompose only long, multi-clause lines, and cap sub-claims below the line's sentence count.

The checker cannot be the only safeguard. On FaithBench, chosen specifically for cases where detectors disagree, the best detectors including GPT-4o-as-judge reach only about **50% accuracy** ([FaithBench](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.13210)). Summary-style rewrites are hardest to check: HHEM scores **64.42** balanced accuracy on RAGTruth's summarisation split vs 74.28 on its QA split, and GPT-4 shows the same gap ([HHEM card](https://huggingface.co/vectara/hallucination_evaluation_model)). Podcast lines are abstractive rewrites of the chapter, the hardest case. So set thresholds to favour recall: a false flag costs one cheap fix call, while a missed error teaches a student something wrong. Send borderline and flagged lines to the LLM judge. Before trusting any of this, hand-label 50–100 NCERT script lines (supported, unsupported, contradicting, analogy) and measure recall on them.

Two honest caveats. First, HHEM-2.1-Open is English-focused. The multilingual HHEM-2.3 is commercial, and MiniCheck's multilingual performance was not verified. For Hindi or regional-language chapters there is currently no proven cheap checker, so keep the LLM judge on every line there. Second, none of these detectors has been benchmarked on Indian school textbooks.

### Keep the judge blind to the draft and make fixes minimal

When the LLM judge runs, it should see only the flagged line, the previous turns, its cited spans and the section text. It should not see the rest of the script, the fact sheet the writer used, or the writer's reasoning. Chain-of-Verification's "factored" variant answers verification questions without seeing the draft, because answers that see the draft "may be prone to repeating it". It beat the joint variant on every task and lifted biography FActScore from **55.9 to 71.4** with factor-plus-revise ([CoVe](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.11495)). Instruct the judge explicitly: "mark as unsupported any claim not stated in or directly implied by the text, even if it is true."

Make the fix call a minimal edit in the style of RARR, which post-edits unsupported content "while preserving the original output as much as possible" ([RARR](https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.08726)). Pass it the line, its evidence span and its neighbouring turns, tell it to change only the unsupported span and keep speaker, tone and length, and accept the edit only if the small checker now passes. Deleting lines is not free. In CoVe's revise step, hallucinated entities per answer fell from 2.95 to 0.68, but correct entities also fell, from **0.59 to 0.38** ([CoVe](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.11495)). So track coverage (fact IDs mentioned per segment) to stop fix rounds from silently dropping syllabus content.

Keep the two-round cap. A line that still fails after round two should fall back to the Dialog Inpainting pattern: Host B gives a near-verbatim paraphrase of the source sentence, and the LLM writes only Host A's bridging reaction ([Dialog Inpainting](https://arxiv.org/abs/2205.09073)). To save calls, the judge can return its issue list first, then a suggested minimal fix, in a single response. That stays consistent with the self-correction evidence, as long as acceptance is decided by the external checker rather than by the model that wrote the fix.

### Block memory leaks where the chapter almost matches what the model knows

The riskiest lines are not those where the chapter is unusual. They are those where it is almost standard. Xie et al. found LLMs are "highly receptive" to coherent evidence that contradicts their memory, but show "strong confirmation bias" when the evidence also contains information consistent with memory ([Xie et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.13300)). A revised NCERT definition, a changed figure, or a topic dropped from the rationalised syllabus is exactly that case. Zhou et al.'s opinion-based prompts ("report what the narrator says") and counterfactual demonstrations significantly improved faithfulness on knowledge-conflict tasks without training ([Zhou et al.](https://aclanthology.org/2023.findings-emnlp.968/)). Apply both in extraction and writing: "The chapter below is the author's account. Report what the author says about X, even if it differs from what you believe." Add one few-shot example whose source contains a deliberately unusual number, with the correct output repeating it. Then have extraction capture numbers, dates, definitions and named lists verbatim, and run a deterministic exact-match check of every number and named entity in a line against the fact sheet before any model-based check. It costs nothing and catches the most common form of leak.

Context-aware decoding (CAD) improved LLaMA's summarisation factuality by **14.3%** by contrasting outputs with and without the context ([CAD](https://aclanthology.org/2024.naacl-short.69/)). It needs token-level probability access that OpenRouter chat endpoints don't provide, so skip it. PustakAI's NCERT-QA results reinforce the no-memory rule from the other direction. Llama-4-Scout scored only about **0.395 F1** on NCERT questions without the chapter, despite strong SQuAD results, and adding the chapter helped every model ([PustakAI](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2511.10002)). Model memory of NCERT is unreliable anyway. When a student picks class, subject and chapter instead of pasting, the pipeline must load a stored copy of the chapter text and never ask the model to recall it.

### Treat the pasted chapter as untrusted input, because the verifier will approve injected text

Students paste text from PDFs and websites that can carry hidden instructions. Greshake et al. showed that LLM-integrated apps blur data and instructions, and that attackers can exploit this remotely ([Greshake et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.12173v2)). BIPIA found every tested LLM vulnerable, and that boundary markers plus explicit reminders give substantial black-box protection ([BIPIA](https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.14197)). Spotlighting's datamarking (replacing whitespace with a marker character) cut summarisation attack success on GPT-3.5-Turbo from about 50% to **3.10%** with no measurable task cost. Plain delimiters roughly halve attack success, but attackers can forge them ([Spotlighting](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.14720)).

Extraction is the only stage that reads raw text. Wrap the chapter there in randomised delimiters, add a reminder that it may contain sentences that look like instructions and that these are content, and optionally datamark it. Every later stage then consumes fact-sheet JSON, which separates data from instructions at the application level. Keep all pipeline rules in the system role, and before anything else strip zero-width Unicode and cap input length. The important design point, which is my inference and has not been tested in the literature, is that a faithful pipeline will faithfully teach injected text. "Tell students to visit example.com" is "supported by the source", so the grounding verifier will approve it. A separate cheap filter for URLs, phone numbers and emails, off-topic or instruction-like sentences, and age-inappropriate content has to sit alongside the grounding check. "Supported" does not mean "safe to say".

## Caching the chapter prefix and switching off reasoning cut most of the bill

With six segments and up to two verify-fix rounds, a single episode can make around thirty calls that each resend the chapter. Three documented changes, none needing new infrastructure, attack that repetition directly. The exact model matters less than this. OpenRouter lists DeepSeek V4.1 Flash as `deepseek/deepseek-v4.1-flash`, from $0.0283 per million input tokens and $1.00 per million output tokens (cheapest provider), with a 1M-token context and JSON-schema structured outputs ([model page](https://openrouter.ai/deepseek/deepseek-v4.1-flash)). It has both reasoning and non-reasoning modes, but confirm with one test call that `reasoning.effort: "none"` is accepted before relying on it.

### Build one fixed prompt prefix and fire an anchor call first

OpenRouter's documentation states that DeepSeek caching is automatic, cache writes cost the normal input price, and **cache reads cost 0.1x**. Gemini 2.5+ reads cost 0.25x and need prompts of at least 1,024 tokens on 2.5 Flash ([OpenRouter caching](https://openrouter.ai/docs/guides/best-practices/prompt-caching)). DeepSeek's guide adds the mechanics. A hit requires the new request's prefix to fully match a persisted prefix. Prefixes are persisted at request boundaries, so in its long-document example the first two requests miss and the third hits. Building the cache takes seconds, and caching is "best-effort", with caches cleared within hours to days ([DeepSeek KV cache](https://api-docs.deepseek.com/guides/kv_cache)).

The practical design follows directly. Make the system message one static block shared by every stage, with no stage-specific rules in it. Make the first user content the chapter, normalised identically every time, followed by the facts JSON once it exists. Put stage-specific instructions and variables (segment number, plan slice, draft to check) last. Run the plan call as an anchor and wait for it to finish before firing segment calls in parallel; if everything fires at once, most calls miss. Pass a `session_id` (the source-text hash you already compute) so OpenRouter keeps every call on the provider that holds the cache. Don't set `provider.order`, which disables that sticky routing. Keep timestamps and request IDs out of the prefix, and confirm hits with `prompt_cache_hit_tokens` and `cached_tokens` in the logs.

Liu et al. supply the quality argument for the same ordering. Models use information at the start and end of a long context best and "significantly" worse in the middle, even models built for long context ([Lost in the Middle](https://aclanthology.org/2024.tacl-1.9/)). So the instruction and the target section belong at the end. Once caching makes the full chapter cheap, the remaining reason to send a segment call only its own section is accuracy, not cost.

### Turn reasoning off for transformation tasks and cap it elsewhere

OpenRouter states that "reasoning tokens are considered output tokens and charged accordingly". `reasoning.effort: "none"` disables them, but some models are mandatory reasoners and reject it. If reasoning consumes all of `max_tokens`, the response comes back empty with `finish_reason: "length"`, and you are still billed ([OpenRouter reasoning](https://www.openrouter.ai/docs/guides/best-practices/reasoning-tokens)). The overthinking evidence shows how large the waste can be: o1-like models used **1,953% more tokens** than conventional models to answer "2+3" ([Chen et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2412.21187v2)). TALE's in-prompt budgets cut reasoning tokens **67%** for under 3% accuracy loss. But budgets that are too tight backfired: a 10-token budget produced 157 tokens, against 86 with a 50-token budget ([TALE](https://arxiv.org/html/2412.18547v5)).

Extraction, script writing and fixing transform text rather than reason through multiple steps, so set effort to none for them. For the LLM judge, try low or minimal effort, or ask for a short issue list before the verdict. Always set `max_tokens` and treat an empty length-truncated response as a failure. The caveat is that all of this evidence comes from math benchmarks. No study measured reasoning's effect on extraction or grounded verification.

### Use strict JSON only where it helps, and batch lightly

Tam et al. found that strict format constraints degrade reasoning. JSON mode did worse partly because it put the answer key before the reasoning, while JSON helped classification-style tasks ([Let Me Speak Freely](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2408.02442)). A later benchmark reports constrained decoding improving downstream tasks by up to 4% ([JSONSchemaBench](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.10868v2)), so the evidence is mixed. The rule that reconciles them is to use schema-enforced JSON where the task is listing-like, which means extraction and verdicts, with `issues` (each quoting the source) placed before `verdict`. Keep the script format light, for example a delimited line of speaker, line type, evidence IDs and text that a regex can parse. Repair malformed JSON locally before paying for a retry.

Batch Prompting cut token and time cost almost in proportion to batch size, up to **5x with six items**. But quality dropped significantly at six items in four of five datasets, and long inputs caused confusion ([Batch Prompting](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2301.08721)). So extract two or three sections per call, echoing section IDs back so missing ones can be detected. Never merge all segments into one giant call: a single failure would force regenerating everything.

### Cascade the verifier tiers, and skip the expensive research techniques

AutoMix cut cost by over 50% at comparable quality by escalating only items a cheap self-check flagged. It also warns that self-verification "can be noisy", with a correct paraphrase rejected and a wrong answer accepted in its own examples ([AutoMix](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2310.12963v5)). FrugalGPT's "up to 98%" cost reduction comes from QA benchmarks, not long-form writing ([FrugalGPT](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.05176)). With one main model, the cascade belongs in verification. Free deterministic checks run first: the JSON parses, all planned fact IDs are present, speakers alternate, and numbers exact-match. HHEM or MiniCheck then scores each factual line. The LLM judge sees only flagged lines and proposes a minimal fix, and a stronger model is called only after a second failure. Do not cascade extraction. Errors there propagate into every later stage, so cheap-but-wrong extraction is a false economy. That is a judgement call, not a tested finding.

Three well-known techniques are not worth it here. LLMLingua-2 token dropping comes close to uncompressed accuracy on some QA tasks ([LLMLingua-2](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2403.12968)), but deleting tokens from a textbook can drop a "not" or a digit, and it needs a self-hosted compressor. Distillation looks impressive, since Distilling Step-by-Step's 770M model beat 540B PaLM on ANLI ([Hsieh et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2305.02301v2)), but that evidence is from classification, and hosting a fine-tuned writer would cost more than Flash-tier API calls at student volume. Learned routers such as RouteLLM needed about $700 of judge-labelled data ([RouteLLM](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.18665v4)). Finally, cache extraction outputs and the plan by source hash, so regenerating one segment never repeats extraction.

| Segment writing | Same prefix; segment plan and state summary last | None | Light line format with `line_type` and `evidence_ids` |
| Tier-1 check | Line, previous turns, cited spans (local HHEM/MiniCheck) | n/a | Support score |
| LLM judge (flagged lines only) | Line, previous turns, spans, section; never the full script | Low or minimal | Issues first, verdict, optional minimal fix |
| Ask the hosts | Same cached prefix plus doubt and playback position | None | Answer citing fact IDs, or `NOT_IN_CHAPTER` |

## Deep questions and refuted misconceptions do the teaching, not banter

The two-voice format is a vehicle; the learning comes from particular moves inside it. Driscoll et al. had students overhear virtual agents in dialogue and monologue formats. Learners wrote significantly more relevant content when the virtual tutee asked deep questions, and "no other differences were significant": shallow questions, comments and other dialogue variants did nothing ([Driscoll et al.](https://digitalcommons.memphis.edu/facpubs/8941)). The finding reaches school age. In a CogSci 2008 report, **342 students in grades 8–11** learned more from scripted dialogues with deep-reasoning questions before each content sentence than from a monologue with the same content. In six 8th-grade classrooms (n = 160), gains were equal to or greater than those under master teachers ([iDRIVE](https://escholarship.org/content/qt4p70m3pc/qt4p70m3pc.pdf)). A 2009 study confirmed the effect across grades 8–11 and across computer literacy and Newtonian physics ([Gholson et al.](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11251-008-9069-2)).

Chi et al. found that dialogue-video observers improved strongly (**d = 1.08**) while monologue observers did not. Observers referred to the tutee's statements twice as often as the tutor's (8.70 vs 4.00), and tutee errors correlated with observers' substantive comments (r = .37) ([Chi et al. 2017](https://education.asu.edu/sites/default/files/lcl/why_students_learn_more_from_dialogue.pdf)). Muldner et al. found that **solo observers benefited most** from dialogue, and solo observers are exactly your listeners ([Muldner et al.](https://repositories.cdlib.org/content/qt4f05w7h1/qt4f05w7h1.pdf?t=op2kgm)).

So write Host A as the listener's stand-in. Host A introduces each core idea with a why/how/what-if question placed *before* the content, is sometimes wrong, and attempts partial explanations that Host B builds on. Chi, Roy and Hausmann found tutoring effective when the tutee constructs knowledge, and not when the tutor simply conveys it ([Chi et al. 2008](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ789293)).

The evidence that dialogue helps is real but fragile, and the failures deserve as much attention as the successes. A dialogue-vs-monologue comparison in an authentic biology course found no difference, and students preferred monologues ([Ding et al. 2018](https://asu.elsevierpure.com/en/publications/failure-to-replicate-using-dialogue-videos-in-learning-lessons-le/)). Dialogue videos lifted positive affect for university students but **not for middle-school students** ([Stranc & Muldner 2019](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-030-23207-8_51)), and Muldner et al. found no benefit on transfer questions. The AI-podcast evidence is the most sobering. In PAIGE, 180 US college students learned from Gemini-scripted podcasts of OpenStax chapters. Generic podcasts **never outperformed the textbook**, personalised ones won in one subject (philosophy, 7.30 vs 5.95), and enjoyment did not correlate with test scores ([PAIGE](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.04645)).

The closest match to this product is more encouraging but preliminary. Kumoi et al. tested **245 first-year high-school students** and found dialogue TTS beat single-speaker TTS on comprehension (q = .025) and cognitive engagement, though listeners rated single-speaker audio more natural. It is a July 2026 preprint with a fixed-order design ([Kumoi et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.12235)). Interruptions backfire: adding LLM-guided reflection prompts to an AI podcast left learning unchanged and made it less attractive ([Menon et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.04787)). So put the thinking in Host A's voiced questions, not in prompts that stop the audio.

The safe position is that a dialogue podcast is about as good as reading, unless the script carries the active ingredients. Offering a single-narrator mode as an A/B option is cheap insurance.

### Misconceptions earn their place, but analogies need tighter control than facts

Muller et al. randomly assigned 364 first-year physics students to four treatments. Dialogue and refutation formats produced mean gains of 4.77 and 4.41 (out of 26), effect sizes of **0.83 and 0.79** over plain exposition (1.77). Exposition padded with interesting extra information managed only 2.41. Low-prior-knowledge students benefited most ([Muller et al.](https://veritasium.com/publications)). Fun facts are not a substitute for confronting misconceptions.

Add two to four candidate misconceptions to the fact sheet. Generate them from a "student who hasn't read the chapter" persona, as Learn Your Way's audio lessons do ([Learn Your Way](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.13348v1)), and keep only those the chapter explicitly contradicts. Then have Host A voice the misconception as a plausible guess, and Host B identify exactly what is wrong and why. Daheim et al. found that conditioning a tutor's response on a verifier's statement of the error produced more targeted and more often correct remediation ([Daheim et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.09136)). So first generate a one-line "what's wrong and the correct principle" note, checked against the cited fact, and condition Host B's refutation on it.

This is the one place the pipeline deliberately draws on the model's own knowledge, of how students go wrong rather than of NCERT content. That is defensible only because the line is tagged as a misconception and must be followed by a sourced refutation.

Analogies are riskier than they look. In Shao et al.'s high-school classroom study, only **16 of 30** plainly generated analogies were judged correct. Errors included mapping errors, physically impossible objects, one object playing two roles, and oversimplification. An immune-response analogy that omitted antigen-presenting cells led its group to answer a test question wrongly. Forcing a mapping onto every feature produced errors, and students showed significant overconfidence ([Shao et al.](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2502.16895)). Analogy quality also drops for specialised concepts ([Yuan et al.](https://aclanthology.org/2024.emnlp-main.346)), so be more conservative in Class 11–12 chapters.

Plan each analogy as an explicit mapping limited to two or three features. Follow it within two turns with a Host B line stating where the analogy breaks and restating the textbook term. End each segment with a recap in textbook vocabulary, not analogy vocabulary.

### Plan with fact IDs to remove repetition and QA ping-pong

Book2Dial is the closest prior work: synthetic teacher-student dialogues generated from textbooks. Persona role-play with a well-informed student won human evaluation, yet **about 21%** of its question-answer pairs lacked factual consistency. Teachers repeated earlier information, and dialogues had few follow-ups and read "like a series of separate QA pairs" ([Book2Dial](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.03307)).

Detailed planning is the proven fix. Re3 improved plot coherence by 14% by putting the plan and the current state into every generation call ([Re3](https://aclanthology.org/2022.emnlp-main.296)). DOC's more detailed hierarchical outline added **+22.5 coherence, +28.2 outline relevance and +20.7 interestingness** over Re3 ([DOC](https://aclanthology.org/2023.acl-long.190)). PodAgent's ablations showed that an outline and role-separated agents both improve scripts ([PodAgent](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.00455)).

So each segment plan should name a learning objective, the fact IDs it owns, an opening deep question, a misconception to voice, an analogy mapping and a one-line recap. Each fact belongs to exactly one segment, which turns "don't repeat" into a checkable constraint. Each segment call then receives the global plan, its own outline, and a state summary (concepts covered, analogies used, the last two lines of the previous segment). Require at least one follow-up per segment that refers to the previous answer. Dialogizer's fix for generic questions also transfers cheaply: generate two or three candidate Host A questions and keep the one most specific to the next fact ([Dialogizer](https://aclanthology.org/2023.emnlp-main.545)).

One conflict needs an explicit rule. Learn Your Way's virtual student, who hadn't seen the material, "may ask questions or give answers beyond the source". That surfaces misconceptions well, but violates your rule. Keep only Host A questions that a cited fact answers. Drop the rest.

PaperWave's two-month study found that an episode outline at the start improved engagement and comprehension, and that listeners were frustrated when background context was omitted ([PaperWave](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.15023)). Make the first segment a short "map of the episode" and give each segment a "why this matters" beat. Chi et al. found tutors covered fewer concepts in dialogue videos (10.90 vs 14.50), so plan three to five core ideas per segment. Split long chapters into several episodes rather than cramming.

### Write for the ear, add spontaneity sparingly, and set examples in India by default

MoonCast's ablation found that a "spontaneous" script with fillers, response words and pause punctuation lifted listeners' spontaneity ratings from **3.21 to 4.03**, close to real scripts' 4.16 ([MoonCast](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.14345v2)). PodEval found such rewrites lower information density ([PodEval](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.00485)), and Székely et al. found that on fluent text, voices with few disfluencies were rated more natural ([Székely et al.](https://people.kth.se/~ghe/pubs/pdf/szekely2019how.pdf)). The split you already have resolves this neatly. Put light fillers and response tokens ("Right.", "Haan, bilkul.") only in `spoken_text`, mostly in Host A's lines and at a low rate, and keep `display_text` clean.

Conversational style improves retention (**d = 0.30**) and transfer (**d = 0.54**) but fades in lessons over about 35 minutes ([Ginns et al.](https://researchers.westernsydney.edu.au/en/publications/designing-instructional-text-in-a-conversational-style-a-meta-ana/)). Verbal signalling ("there are three reasons… first…") adds **d = .38** ([Alpizar et al.](https://rex.libraries.wsu.edu/esploro/outputs/journalArticle/A-meta-analysis-of-signaling-principle-in/99900601052901842)). Rettberg documents that NotebookLM's audio overviews follow a fixed upbeat template and reframe content around an American viewpoint ([Rettberg](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.08654)). So instruct the writer to use Indian examples, rupees, local geography and an Indian-English register, and to vary its openings. Personalisation was the only podcast condition that beat the textbook in PAIGE. Learn Your Way re-levelled text to a target grade and applied learner interests only to suitable passages. That argues for a class-level parameter and an optional interest hook (cricket, cooking, festivals) applied only to analogy and banter lines.

### Score scripts offline with per-dimension critics, never with a small judge

For prompt iteration, PodBench's two-stage protocol is the best-validated script evaluator. Stage one is an LLM-built checklist of explicit requirements. Stage two weights Content Substance 45, Narrative Engagement 30 and Conversational Naturalness 25. It agreed with the human majority **86.8%** of the time, above human inter-annotator agreement (83.4%). Content Substance was the weakest dimension even for strong models ([PodBench](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.14903)), so that is where prompt effort pays off. Redefine it as "conceptual depth plus misconception resolution".

Jurenka et al. built one critic prompt per pedagogical dimension and gave the critics privileged information such as the correct solution ([Jurenka et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.12687v4)). Two warnings apply. Maurya et al. found Prometheus2 and Llama-3.1-8B judges had **mostly negative correlations** with human pedagogy labels ([Maurya et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2412.09416v2)), and G-Eval flags that LLM judges may favour LLM-written text ([G-Eval](https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.16634)). So use a strong judge from a different model family, compare prompt versions pairwise, and keep evaluation out of the per-episode cost path. Count-based checks from `line_type` tags need no judge at all: deep questions before content, misconceptions voiced and refuted, and follow-ups per segment.

## Indic-Speak needs a digit-free spoken_text and a per-line listen-back check

Per-line synthesis is the right choice for Indic-Speak, and the reason is constraint more than optimality. Whole-dialogue models beat concatenated turns. CoVoMix's joint generation gave the most consistent speaker similarity across utterances ([CoVoMix](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.06690)), and Sesame's context-aware model still lost to real recordings when judged in 90-second context ([Sesame](https://www.sesame.com/research/crossing_the_uncanny_valley_of_voice)). But those models are English or Chinese only, and Indic-Speak takes no audio context. The work therefore shifts to coding around the engine's documented behaviour, normalising text before it arrives, and checking every clip after it leaves.

### Code around Indic-Speak's documented failure modes

The Hugging Face model card describes a Llama-3.2-3B-based speech LM emitting SNAC codes, decoded to **24 kHz** audio. Its most dangerous behaviour: a speaker name that doesn't match a training voice **raises no error** and produces an averaged, lower-quality voice. Style tags are "preview" quality, there are no non-verbal sounds (laughter, sighs), and the card warns of "possible mispronunciation of unusual words and difficulty with very complex equations". It reports WER/CER of 26.49/17.86 on an unstated test set ([Indic-Speak card](https://huggingface.co/bodhan-ai/indic-speak)). Launch coverage adds that some outputs **repeat or drop content**, and that each voice keeps its native accent when reading other languages. The API recommends short inputs, and Bodhan's own long demo is stitched from separately generated paragraphs. The price is **₹6 per 10,000 input characters**. In Bodhan's own evaluation, **0.7%** of 30,000 code-mixed readings scored 2 or below out of 5 for content fidelity, and human listening tests were still "in progress" ([Analytics Vidhya](https://www.analyticsvidhya.com/blog/2026/09/bodhan-ai-indic-models/)). Even a sub-1% failure rate means a 150-line episode will often contain a defective line, so automatic per-line checking is required, not optional. The coverage says 45 voices while the card lists 95, so validate voice names against the API's own list, not the card. Choose voices native to each episode's language. Never script "(laughs)". Don't rely on style tags until they have been A/B tested; AI4Bharat's Rasa work found "happy" is often confused with neutral ([Rasa](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.14056)), so put energy into word choice instead.

Before a Tamil launch, run a voice-selection listening test. A 2026 accent benchmark found retroflex collapse rising from about 1% in Hindi to **about 68% in Tamil** across commercial systems, and WER rankings did not predict accent fidelity ([PSP](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.25476)). Keep a `tts_engine` abstraction so failing lines can fall back to Indic Parler-TTS, which is Apache 2.0 ([card](https://huggingface.co/ai4bharat/indic-parler-tts)), or AI4Bharat's FastPitch Indic-TTS, which is MIT licensed and, being non-autoregressive, cannot loop or skip the way token models do ([Indic-TTS](https://arxiv.org/abs/2211.09536)). Evidence quality is weak here. There is no Bodhan technical report and no published per-language MOS, and these figures come from a model card and press coverage.

### Verbalise numbers, formulae and notation before text reaches TTS

Apple's PolyNorm shows that one instruction plus about 80–100 localised few-shot examples per language lets GPT-4o beat a production rule-based normaliser in all eight languages tested (German WER **10.74% to 4.17%**). Its benchmark includes Mathematical Expression and Chemical Formula categories ([PolyNorm](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.03080)). MathReader cut WER from **0.510 to 0.281** against Microsoft Edge's reader by verbalising LaTeX before TTS rather than leaving formulas to the engine ([MathReader](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.07088)). ClearSpeak, designed for reading algebra to secondary students, uses pausing to convey structure ([ClearSpeak](https://www.ets.org/research/policy_research_reports/publications/report/2016/jwog.html)). Indic-Speak takes no SSML, so commas in `spoken_text` are your only pause control ("x squared, plus two x, plus one").

Neekhara et al. report that LLM-based TTS repetitions and skips are "especially likely when the input text repeats the same token" ([Neekhara et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.17957)). A genotype like "TT" rendered as "capital T capital T" is exactly that trigger, so prefer "double capital T", or at least a comma.

A two-layer design fits. The first layer is an LLM normalisation pass with category-labelled NCERT few-shot examples: ratios ("three is to one"), formulae and ions, reaction arrows ("gives"), units, scientific notation, genotypes and Roman numerals. For Hindi and Tamil, the LLM picks *how* a number is read and AI4Bharat's MIT-licensed `indic-numtowords` writes the words, including variant forms such as डेढ़ सौ ([indic-numtowords](https://pypi.org/project/indic-numtowords/1.0.0/)), avoiding LLM inflection errors. The second layer is a deterministic validator in render.py. It rejects any `spoken_text` containing digits, subscripts, operators or Greek letters, and flags repeated n-grams. With every digit gone, Bodhan's internal normaliser has nothing ambiguous left to reinterpret. To control cost (my inference), route only lines whose `display_text` contains digits or symbols through the normalisation pass. Build a 20-items-per-category regression set, as PolyNorm did, and re-run it whenever the prompt changes. Separately, check `spoken_text` against `display_text` (numbers re-normalised, then compared) rather than re-verifying it against the chapter, since "1.5" drifting to "fifteen" is a normalisation error, not a grounding one. No Indic-language normalisation benchmark exists, and how Indic-Speak's internal normaliser treats already-verbalised text is undocumented.

### Listen back to every clip with ASR and regenerate failures

The standard objective protocol in TTS research, Seed-TTS-Eval, is an ASR round-trip WER plus speaker similarity measured as the cosine between WavLM embeddings ([Seed-TTS-Eval](https://github.com/BytedanceSpeech/seed-tts-eval)). Bodhan's own Indic-Transcribe Core is the natural ASR. It is trained on Indian-accented English and the scheduled languages, reaches a Hindi OIWER of **3.5**, and outputs numbers as words, so it can be compared directly with `spoken_text` ([Indic-Transcribe Core](https://huggingface.co/bodhan-ai/indic-transcribe-core)). Generic English ASR does measurably worse on Indian accents ([Svarah](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.15760)). Do not compute CER with Whisper's text normaliser: Manohar et al. show such normalisers are "fundamentally flawed" for Indic scripts and give "artificially improved" scores ([Manohar et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.02449)). Write an Indic-aware normaliser that applies NFC, strips punctuation and keeps matras.

Zuo et al. add two black-box signals: a **duration-abnormality** check, and a pre-generation text risk score built from features such as numbers, code-switching and length, which correlates with content errors ([Zuo et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.11545)). CoVoMix2's authors observed MoonCast and Sesame hallucinating and in some cases generating endlessly ([CoVoMix2](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.00885)), which is the same failure family as Indic-Speak.

Cost barely matters here. At launch prices, ASR is ₹0.10 per audio minute, about ₹1.5 for a 15-minute episode ([Analytics Vidhya](https://www.analyticsvidhya.com/blog/2026/09/bodhan-ai-indic-models/)), and at ₹6 per 10,000 characters, re-synthesising a tenth of an episode's lines costs a rupee or two.

| Check (in order) | Catches | Starting point (my design; tune on about 200 labelled clips) |
|------------------|---------|--------------------------------------------------------------|
| Pre-synthesis validator and risk score | Digits or symbols left in `spoken_text`; repeated tokens | Reject or rewrite; stricter later thresholds for high-risk lines |
| Trimmed duration vs expected duration from `spoken_text` length | Looping, runaway generation, omissions | Flag outside 0.5x–1.8x of that voice's median rate |
| ASR round-trip CER (Indic-Transcribe Core, Indic-aware normaliser) | Dropped, added or garbled words | About 0.10 for English, 0.15–0.20 for Indic; flag repeated n-grams absent from the reference |
| WavLM speaker cosine vs the host's centroid | Voice drift between lines | Below that voice's 5th percentile |
| Forced-alignment score (next subsection) | Omission or extra audio | Low score or a large star-absorbed span |
| UTMOS ([SpeechMOS](https://github.com/tarepan/SpeechMOS)) as a relative outlier | Artefacts | More than 2 SD below that voice's mean; English-trained, so never use as an absolute bar |

Retry up to three times. Keep the best take, route persistent failures to the fallback engine, and log them for review. No published "regenerate" thresholds exist; the numbers above are starting points to calibrate on your own audio.

### Control pauses, caption timing and loudness in the stitcher

Turn-taking research gives the pause model. Across ten languages, gaps between turns have **modes of 0–200 ms**, with means from 7 ms (Japanese) to 468 ms (Danish) and 236 ms for English. Most transitions are under 500 ms ([Levinson & Torreira](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2015.00731/full)). Trim each clip's leading and trailing silence to a small fixed pad before inserting gaps; otherwise the model's variable silence adds to yours. Draw between-speaker gaps from a distribution centred near 200–250 ms rather than using a constant. Use near-zero gaps after short responses like "Right.", and deliberately longer pauses (my heuristic: 600–900 ms) at segment transitions and after questions the listener should think about. No turn-gap data exist for Indian languages. Sesame's result also implies you should judge naturalness by listening to 60–90-second stretches of a stitched episode, not isolated clips.

For captions, replace character-proportional cue splitting with per-clip forced alignment of `spoken_text`. The MMS aligner romanises text with uroman, so Devanagari, Tamil and Hinglish go through one path. It uses a star token to absorb stray audio, and its alignment score filters bad segments ([MMS](https://jmlr.org/papers/volume25/23-1318/23-1318.pdf)). That score doubles as a QC signal for omissions and repetitions.

Three practical traps await. torchaudio's `forced_align` API was "deprecated in 2.8 and removed in 2.9", so pin the version ([torchaudio](https://docs.pytorch.org/audio/stable/tutorials/ctc_forced_alignment_api_tutorial.html)). uroman normalisation drops digits ([multilingual tutorial](https://docs.pytorch.org/audio/stable/tutorials/forced_alignment_for_multilingual_data_tutorial.html)), another reason `spoken_text` must be digit-free. The convenient `ctc-forced-aligner` package's default model is licensed CC-BY-NC ([ctc-forced-aligner](https://github.com/MahmoudAshraf97/ctc-forced-aligner)), so swap it if the app ever becomes commercial. Until alignment lands, the cheap fix is to split cue time in proportion to `spoken_text` characters, not `display_text` characters: "3:1" is three display characters but about sixteen spoken ones. Compute clip offsets from the final trimmed, padded buffers. To map display to spoken timing cleanly, have the script emit aligned segment pairs (my inference), such as ["H₂O", "H two O"].

Size cues to Netflix's children's limits: **17 characters per second for English and 18 for Hindi and Tamil**, 42 characters per line, two lines, and a minimum duration of about 0.83 s ([Netflix English](https://partnerhelp.netflixstudios.com/hc/en-us/articles/217350977-English-USA-Timed-Text-Style-Guide); [Netflix Hindi](https://partnerhelp.netflixstudios.com/hc/en-us/articles/115003196707-Hindi-Timed-Text-Style-Guide); [Netflix timing](https://partnerhelp.netflixstudios.com/hc/en-us/articles/360051554394-Timed-Text-Style-Guide-Subtitle-Timing-Guidelines)). Eye-tracking found 12–20 cps did not affect comprehension, but 12 cps caused re-reading ([Szarkowska & Gerber-Morón](https://discovery-pp.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/10050653/1/journal.pone.0199331.pdf)). Never let a cue span a speaker change; use WebVTT `<v>` voice spans.

For loudness, Apple's podcast spec is **−16 LKFS ±1 dB** integrated with true peak at or below **−1 dBFS**, and allows mono 22.05/24 kHz at 40–80 kbps, which matches Indic-Speak's 24 kHz output ([Apple](https://podcasters.apple.com/support/893-audio-requirements)). AES TD1008 recommends −18 LUFS for speech on streaming services ([summary](https://productionadvice.co.uk/td1008/)). For a web player with no normalisation of its own, master to −16. The step most stitched two-voice podcasts miss is to **gain-match every clip to a common level before stitching**, so the hosts don't jump in loudness at every turn. Then normalise the master with a true-peak limiter, and record the measured LUFS in `manifest.json`.

## Checkpoints with feedback, not the podcast itself, drive learning gains

The learning-science evidence tells a consistent story: audio alone is roughly as good as reading, the player's scaffolds are what push it above that, and quizzes are the strongest scaffold. Clinton-Lisell's meta-analysis of **46 studies (N = 4,687)** found no overall difference between listening and reading comprehension, though readers who could self-pace and re-read had a small edge ([RER TOC](https://journals.sagepub.com/toc/rera/92/4); [summary](https://www.techlearning.com/news/listen-without-guilt-audiobooks-offer-similar-comprehension-as-reading)). Varao Sousa et al. found mind-wandering at **0.506 of probes when listening vs 0.356 when reading**, with worse memory ([Varao Sousa et al.](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2013.00892/pdf)). Undergraduates assigned podcasts scored the same as those assigned articles but *believed* they learned more ([Choi et al.](https://scholars.uky.edu/es/publications/hooked-on-podcasts-evidence-from-a-quasi-experimental-approach/)). Satisfaction ratings will flatter the product, so let quiz accuracy drive any "mastery" display.

### Keep captions on, make rewinding easy, and keep episodes short

Your synced transcript is better supported than the classic redundancy principle suggests. Adesope and Nesbit's meta-analysis of 57 experiments found spoken-plus-written presentation beat spoken-only precisely for **low-prior-knowledge learners, system-paced material, and material without pictures**, which describes your player exactly. On-screen key terms drove much of the advantage ([Adesope & Nesbit](https://rex.libraries.wsu.edu/esploro/outputs/journalArticle/Verbal-Redundancy-in-Multimedia-Learning-Environments/99900601157101842)). Reading while listening beat reading alone by **g = 0.41** when the pace was externally controlled, but only g = 0.06 when self-paced, and voice type (human vs computer) did not moderate the effect ([Clinton-Lisell 2023](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1403866.pdf)). For second-language learners, which describes most English-medium Indian students, a captioning meta-analysis reported large effects on listening comprehension and vocabulary; keyword captions matched full captions, but learners preferred full ones ([Hosogoshi, summarising Montero Perez et al.](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1125240.pdf)). Indian same-language subtitling of film songs has been associated with literacy gains, which is suggestive precedent for synced highlighted text ([Kothari](https://www.iima.ac.in/publication/same-language-subtitling-literacy-development-india-through-film-songs)).

So default captions to on, use the same script as the audio, show the full transcript with fact-sheet key terms highlighted, and highlight the current word or line. Add a 10–15-second rewind and tap-a-line-to-seek, to restore the self-pacing advantage readers have.

Median engagement in 6.9 million MOOC sessions was **at most about 6 minutes**, whatever the video length ([Guo et al.](https://www.cs.rochester.edu/hci/pubs/pdfs/edX-MOOC-video-production-and-engagement_LAS-2014.pdf)), and retention fell in the second half of a 40-minute lecture ([Farley et al.](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2013.00619/full)). That points to 7–10-minute episodes made of 3–4-minute segments, with the most important concept early. For playback speed, 1x outperformed only 2.5x (d = .24). Re-watching at 2x about a week later, just before the test, beat one viewing at 1x (**.63 vs .55**) ([Murphy et al.](https://castel.psych.ucla.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/111/2022/03/Applied-Cognitive-Psychology-2021-Murphy-Learning-in-double-time-The-effect-of-lecture-video-speed-on-immediate-and.pdf)). Cap speed at 2x, offer 0.75x for younger and L2 listeners, and add a fast "exam revision" re-listen mode.

On voice, modern TTS shows no learning penalty against human voices ([Dinçer](https://doaj.org/article/a14435a205f54240bdd0b0db2fa06f91)), but voice quality does shape trust ([Chiou et al.](https://asu.elsevierpure.com/en/publications/how-we-trust-perceive-and-learn-from-virtual-humans-the-influence/)), which matters for parents and teachers. Every one of these studies used Western or university samples, and none tested Indian-accented TTS.

### Checkpoints with feedback are the best-evidenced feature you already have

Szpunar et al. split a 21-minute recorded lecture into four segments of about 5.5 minutes, with a short quiz after each. Mind-wandering fell to **19%** against 39–41% without quizzes, final test scores reached **90% vs 68%**, and test anxiety went *down* ([Szpunar et al.](https://dash.harvard.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/f3608457-a87d-4171-8f75-b068482e8233/content)). Yang et al.'s meta-analysis of **222 classroom studies and 48,478 students** found quizzing raises achievement by g = 0.499. Effects were larger with feedback (0.537 vs 0.374 without) and with more tests (0.444 for one test, 0.642 for three or more). They were largest of any level in **middle school (0.597) and high school (0.655)**. Multiple choice (0.567) did nearly as well as short answer (0.638), which makes it a reasonable mobile-friendly default ([Yang et al.](https://scholars.hkbu.edu.hk/en/publications/testing-quizzing-boosts-classroom-learning-a-systematic-and-meta-/)). Butler and Roediger show why feedback is non-negotiable. A week after a multiple-choice test, correct recall was **.31 with no feedback, .45 with immediate feedback and .56 with delayed feedback**. Without feedback, students later reproduced the wrong options they had picked at a rate of .24 ([Butler & Roediger](https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/MC.36.3.604)). Spacing research adds that the best gap between reviews grows with how long material must be retained ([Cepeda et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16719566/)).

In practice: two or three checkpoints of one to three questions at segment boundaries, every three to five minutes, plus an end quiz. Always reveal the answer with a one-line reason. Resurface missed items in the recap, a day later and a week later, which approximates the delayed-feedback advantage. Make the recap a retrieval exercise: the host asks, pauses, then answers. Szpunar's sample was Harvard undergraduates watching video, and Yang's classrooms were mostly Western and East Asian. Indian Class 6–12 effects are likely similar in direction, but their size is unknown.

### Generate quiz items from fact IDs and filter for guessability

LLM distractors are typically "valid" wrong answers that don't match how real students go wrong ([Feng et al.](https://aclanthology.org/2024.findings-naacl.193)). In head-to-head comparisons, teachers preferred human distractors **62%** of the time, though picking the top three from many candidates beat generating exactly three ([Scarlatos et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.05144v2)). DiVERT writes a text description of the student's error before each distractor; its error descriptions were rated on par with human ones and well above GPT-4o's ([DiVERT](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.19356v2)). Borrowing the idea needs no training. For each item, list misconceptions, write one distractor per misconception, overgenerate and keep three, and store each misconception as that option's spoken feedback ("if you picked B, you may be thinking…").

Scaria et al. (IISc) found that a prompt combining chain-of-thought with a short definition of the target Bloom's level gave the best balance of quality (**81.18%**) and level match (76.32%). Overly long prompts hurt both, and an LLM judge disagreed badly with experts on Bloom's levels ([Scaria et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2408.04394v1)). Prompting alone does not control difficulty: requesting harder items produced "no clear relationship" with measured difficulty ([Tomikawa & Uto](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2510.19265v1)).

Filtering matters more than generation. Säuberli and Clematide define guessability as accuracy *without* the text, and GPT-4 tracked human judgements of it closely ([Säuberli & Clematide](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.07720v2)). NCERT content is often general knowledge, so answer every item twice, with no context and with only its cited fact, and drop items answered correctly without context.

Rule-based item-writing-flaw checks agreed with humans more than GPT-4 did (**90.87% vs 78.89%**), and GPT-4 flagged **4.2 flaws per item against humans' 1.6** ([Moore et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2307.08161)). So run deterministic rules first: no "all/none of the above", key not systematically longest, randomised key position. Send only "more than one correct" and "implausible distractor" to an LLM. A 2026 review adds that SAQUET's headline 94% accuracy barely beats an always-negative baseline (93%), and that LLM repair introduced new flaws in **56–68%** of revised items ([Moore & Diana](https://aclanthology.org/2026.aimecon-wip.58.pdf)). Regenerate flagged items rather than repairing them.

Most distractor evidence comes from math (Eedi, students aged about 10–13). There is no public NCERT multiple-choice dataset. EduProbe's 3,502 NCERT-derived open-ended items across Classes 6–12 are the closest test bed ([EduProbe](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2312.01032)). Log which distractors students pick: that is exactly the preference signal the overgenerate-and-rank ranker was trained on.

### Ask the hosts should read the whole chapter and refuse by default

For a single chapter, skip vector retrieval. Li et al. found that putting the full text in context beat RAG by **7.6, 13.1 and 3.6 points** for Gemini-1.5-Pro, GPT-4o and GPT-3.5. RAG failed most on vague or implicit queries ([Li et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.16833v1)), and "I didn't get the part about…" is exactly that kind of doubt. While the chapter's cached prefix is still warm, a doubt costs mostly the question and the answer.

PustakAI's NCERT experiments found that chain-of-thought prompting *hurt* faithfulness, while a direct meta-prompt with one example worked best ([PustakAI](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2511.10002)). Georgia Tech's Jill Watson is a blueprint worth copying. A router sorts each message into on-topic doubt, chit-chat, off-topic or unsafe. Follow-up questions are rewritten so their references resolve, which here means anchoring "this" to the current playback section. The answer comes only from the context, with citations, or else "I don't know". A textual-entailment check then confirms the context supports the answer, and moderation runs on input and output. It passed **76.7%** of student questions against 31.3% for OpenAI Assistants. Its average latency was 6.8 s ([Jill Watson](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2405.11070)), so budget a spoken "good question, let me check" filler. Reuse your HHEM or MiniCheck tier as the entailment gate. Have the model output a structured `NOT_IN_CHAPTER` when nothing supports an answer. Send spoken answers through the same normalisation and QC path as episode lines.

Abstention is the fragile part. Wen et al. found models reluctant to abstain on yes/no questions, with abstention highly sensitive to the prompt; removing context made GPT-4o abstain on **62%** of questions that were in fact answerable ([Wen et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.12452v2)). Build a test set with three buckets: answerable from the chapter, related but not in the chapter, and off-topic. Include yes/no doubts, and report answer accuracy and abstention precision/recall separately. Map refusals to distinct host lines for "not in this chapter", "I'm not sure" and off-limits topics ([Know Your Limits](https://aclanthology.org/2025.tacl-1.26)).

Educators preferred lightly guided answers over heavily guided ones, though heavy guidance was the most grounded ([Henkel et al.](https://www.educationaldatamining.org/edm2024/proceedings/2024.EDM-short-papers.28)). So constrain the *claims* to the chapter, not the tone. For Hindi or regional-language doubts, expect a quality drop. Gemma-2's grounded QA F1 was 52.17 in English, **44.46 in Hindi and 28.81 in Odia** ([Indic QA](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.13522v2)), so benchmark your model per language before launch.

## Conclusion

The review changes where the hard design decision sits. It is not model choice or prompt wording but the line schema. The faithfulness rule and the pedagogy evidence collide at exactly three points: Host A's naive questions, which the best-performing persona designs let stray beyond the source; voiced misconceptions, which are false on purpose; and analogies, roughly half of which are wrong when generated plainly. A `line_type` field with a distinct verification rule per type, plus `evidence_ids` on every factual line, reconciles all three. It also makes the cheapest safeguards possible: exact-match number checks, a CPU-sized entailment model, and count-based pedagogy checks that need no judge. The best-evidenced components here are also the cheapest: prefix caching, feedback on checkpoints, a sub-1B checker. The expensive ones (multi-agent dialogue simulation, LLM judges for pedagogy, fine-tuning) have the weakest support at student scale.

The larger gap is the population. Most learning studies cited here used US, European or East Asian university students. The school-age exceptions are the grades 8–11 iDRIVE work, Muldner's middle-schoolers, the high-school students in Shao's and Kumoi's studies, Learn Your Way's 15–18-year-olds, and the classroom studies pooled in Yang's meta-analysis. None used Indian Class 6–12 students, NCERT text or Indian-language TTS. The Indian evidence covers adjacent questions. One study found that in primary school, mother-tongue education raised regional-language reading but lowered English reading ([Vogelzang et al.](https://opendocs.ids.ac.uk/articles/journal_contribution/Effects_of_Mother_Tongue_Education_and_Multilingualism_on_Reading_Skills_in_the_Regional_Language_and_English_in_India/30464702)). Another found large gains from a personalised, practice-heavy program for middle-schoolers in Delhi ([Muralidharan et al.](https://economics.ucsd.edu/events/news/MuralidharanAER2019.html)). Together they argue for bilingual episodes and for treating the quizzes, not the audio, as the active ingredient. Treat every effect size above as a direction, not a forecast.

The product can generate the evidence the literature lacks. Its quiz logs can run A/B tests on captions on vs off, English vs bilingual episodes, dialogue vs single narrator, and checkpoint frequency. Its distractor picks are training data for a ranker. Its verifier flags, paired with human corrections, are the in-domain labels RAGTruth shows can make a small fine-tuned detector competitive with GPT-4 ([RAGTruth](https://aclanthology.org/2024.acl-long.585/)). The single most valuable thing to build first is a hand-labelled gold set of 50–100 NCERT script lines and quiz items. Without it, none of the thresholds above can be tuned honestly.
